Time Machine

The Invention of Writing: Before and After

The history of humanity stretches back millions of years, but the way we study it changed completely with the invention of writing.

Before Writing vs. After Writing

  • Before Writing: Covers more than 99% of human history (from ~3 million to 5000 years ago). The main sources for reconstruction are tools, implements, and material artefacts. It is very difficult to understand the thoughts and ideas of people, and measurement of time/dating is only approximate.
  • After Writing: Covers less than 1% of history (the last 5000 years). Reconstructed using both material remains and written documents. Literature reveals names, events, and social/political life. Dating becomes relatively accurate because documents mention specific dates like coronations and wars.

Harappan Script (Sindhu-Sarasvatī): One of the earliest known systems. They used a pictographic script (Sindhu lipi) found on seals and pottery. However, it has not yet been deciphered, so their exact thoughts remain a mystery.

Deciphered Scripts: The cuneiform script (Mesopotamia) and hieroglyphic script (Egypt) flourished at the same time as the Harappans. Both have been deciphered, marking the start of the historical period about 5000 years ago.

Brahmi Script: Used from about 400 BCE in southern India and the Ganga Valley. It was later formalised by Emperor Ashoka in the 3rd century BCE.

Bronze Age Civilisations Outside India

The shift to producing surplus food and material goods laid the foundation for the earliest civilizations, which all emerged in fertile river plains.

The Four River Plains

  • Sindhu-Sarasvatī Civilisation: Along the Sindhu and Sarasvatī rivers.
  • Mesopotamian Civilisation: Around the Euphrates and Tigris rivers in West Asia.
  • Egyptian Civilisation: Along the Nile River.
  • Chinese Civilisation: In the Huang He basin in northern China.

Geographic Note: Mesopotamia and the Indus valleys were geographically close, facilitating strong contacts and trade. Conversely, there is little tangible evidence of direct contact between Egypt/China and the Sindhu-Sarasvatī civilisation.

Mesopotamia: The Land in Between

Mesopotamia is the Greek name for the land drained by the Euphrates and Tigris rivers. Today, this covers Iraq, Kuwait, and parts of Turkey and southwestern Iran. The crescent-shaped foothills from the Mediterranean to the Persian Gulf are called the 'fertile crescent'. Farming began here 12,000 years ago, and copper tools arrived around 4500 BCE.

The earliest city-based civilisation (cities like Ur in southern Iraq). They were the first to build dams and canals for irrigation, and use mud/burnt bricks for houses and defensive walls.

  • Religion & Society: Worshipped multiple gods controlling floods and winds. They built a grand stepped pyramid-shaped temple called a ziggurat. The top was holy, surrounded by palaces and treasuries, with only one entrance. Entry was restricted to high priests/priestesses. Kings lived in palaces; regular people lived in small brick houses.
  • Economy: Focused on farming, metalworking, pottery, textiles, and merchants.
  • Innovations: Invented the wheeled cart, sailboat, and a number system based on 60 (giving us the 60-minute hour, 60-second minute, and 360-degree circle). Used math for building and measuring fields.
  • Writing (3200 BCE): Invented cuneiform. Scribes used wedge-shaped reeds on damp clay tablets. Scribes held high status like priests. Used for myths (Epic of Gilgamesh), hymns, laws, and tracking work of potters, seal cutters, shipbuilders, carpenters, and farmers. Used across different city-states despite language differences.

Emerged in central Mesopotamia. Though they spoke Akkadian, they used the Sumerian cuneiform script. Under King Sargon, they established the world's first dynastic empire and evolved creative literature.

Trade: Traded with Dilmun (Bahrain), Magan (Oman), and Meluhha (Sindhu-Sarasvatī). They exchanged semiprecious stone beads, ivory, timber, gold dust, and copper.

Centered in the northern city-state of Assur. Their dominance rapidly spread across Mesopotamia, the west, and the south, lasting until early 1700 BCE.

Gained dominance in central Mesopotamia. Reached peak glory under King Hammurabi (1792 BCE), who compiled the Code of Hammurabi—rules for civil and social conduct that became a model for future legal systems.

Decline: Lost prominence by 1400 BCE due to attacks by the Hittites (who mastered new technologies/natural resources), environmental degradation, pressure on agricultural lands, and internal political/economic problems.

Egyptian Civilisation (3000 BCE)

Flourished along the river Nile. The land was called Kemet ('the black') because of the rich mud left by the Nile's annual summer floods.

Greeks like Herodotus visited in the 5th century BCE, and a 19th-century collection called de L'Egypte provides fascinating details.

Farmers dug ditches and stored water in reservoirs. This collective effort led to local governments and an administrative class. They created a calendar based on the rising of Sirius (Dog Star) and counting days between floods.

The Calendar: 3 seasons (Inundation/autumn, Peret growing/winter, Shemu harvest/summer). Year = 365 days (12 months x 30 days + 5 extra days). It lost time because the real year is 1/4 day longer.

Papyrus: "Old paper" made by cutting the inner trunk of the papyrus plant into strips, criss-crossing, pressing, drying, and polishing them. (e.g., Papyrus Ebers lists 700+ cures/spells).

Libraries (2000 BCE): Stored scrolls in labeled jars. Stories included Sindbad, animal fables (linked to Aesop), ghosts, miracles, romances, and the oldest form of Cinderella.

The Rosetta Stone: Found in 1799 by French engineer Pierre Bouchard while repairing a fort. A black stone with three scripts (including Greek). Deciphered in 1822 by French linguist Jean-François Champollion.

Social Hierarchy: Pharaoh -> Gov Officials, Nobles, Priests -> Free Landholders, Artisans, Merchants -> Serfs and Slaves.

Burial: Believed every person had a ka (spiritual double) that lived on if the body was preserved via mummification (remove organs except heart, dry with natron, oil, wrap in linen strips, place in coffin). The Papyrus of Ani shows the "weighing of the heart".

Tombs: Pharaohs were buried underground covered by a rectangular structure called a mastaba. These were stacked to form pyramids, like the step pyramid at Saqqara.

Leisure: Swam, canoed, played board games, music, and dancing. Festivals paraded god statues to music from a sistrum. The Sed festival marked a king's 30th year on the throne.

Women: Egyptian women enjoyed more rights than Greek or Roman women; they could own property and run businesses. Cleopatra (69-30 BCE) was trained to rule from childhood and became queen at 18.
